My grandmother kept a diary for sixty years, and when she died my mother found forty notebooks in a cardboard box under her bed. Reading them taught me more about why people keep diaries than any essay could. The first reason is memory. We think we will remember the day we moved house or the name of the neighbour who helped carry the boxes, but details fade within months. A diary holds them still. The second reason is thinking. Writing a sentence forces you to decide what you actually feel, and my grandmother often changed her mind halfway down a page. You can watch her argue with herself about whether to take a job in the city. The third reason is company. On evenings when nobody called, the notebook listened, and it never repeated anything. Some people say diaries are old-fashioned now that phones record everything, but a photo shows where you were, not what you were afraid of. I started my own diary last spring. Most entries are short and boring, a line about the weather or a test, yet rereading them already surprises me, because the person who wrote them was worrying about things I have completely forgotten.
