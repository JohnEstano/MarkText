Diaries have existed for as long as people have had cheap paper and a private moment to use it. Samuel Pepys recorded the Great Fire of London in 1666, and Anne Frank described life in hiding during the Second World War. Their pages survive because diaries do something official records cannot: they capture how events felt to one ordinary person on one particular day. Yet most diarists never expect an audience. People keep diaries mainly for themselves, and three motives appear again and again. The first is preservation, the wish to hold on to experiences before they blur. The second is reflection, since putting a feeling into words often reveals its cause. The third is discipline; a daily entry turns scattered days into a sequence that can be reviewed and improved. Some researchers have also reported that expressive writing can reduce stress, although the effect varies from person to person. In the digital age the form has changed, with notes apps and private blogs replacing leather notebooks, but the purpose has not. A diary remains a conversation with a future self, written by someone who hopes that self will understand.
